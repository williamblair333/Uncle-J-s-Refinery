# Brandon Sanderson Style

Write the way Sanderson writes. Don't write his books. Use his craft, never his content:
no Allomancy, Surgebinding, Investiture, Cosmere names, Shards, spren, Kandra or any
other term or character he invented. Build original equivalents that follow the same rules.

## The voice: "window-pane" prose

The prose is a clear window onto the story, not stained glass. The reader should see
the events, not the sentences.

- Plain, precise words. Use a metaphor only when it explains something, never as decoration.
- Use tight third-person limited. Each chapter stays in one head, and the narration takes on that
  character's vocabulary, worries and sense of humor.
- Keep paragraphs short and keep the scene moving. Interiority comes as quick, specific thoughts,
  not long reflection.
- Explain clearly. When a mechanic matters, the POV character thinks it through in plain
  terms, so the reader understands enough to predict what happens.
- Humor comes from character: dry asides, banter, a character's absurd but sincere habits.
  Keep it warm, not cynical.
- Light profanity, often invented in-world oaths. Violence is kinetic but not gory.
- Avoid ornate lyricism, long sentences built for rhythm, ambiguity for its own sake, and
  narrative sarcasm aimed at the characters.

## Magic and worldbuilding: Sanderson's Laws

1. **Understanding buys solutions.** Magic can resolve a conflict only as far as the
   reader understands it. Explain the rules before the climax uses them. Don't spring a
   new power at the climax.
2. **Limits beat powers.** Costs, weaknesses and limits make magic interesting.
   Every ability needs a price, such as fuel, physical toll, a narrow trigger or a rule about who
   can use it. Characters win through clever use inside the limits.
3. **Expand before adding.** Go deeper into an existing system (new uses, new combinations,
   consequences for society) before you invent a new one.
- **Zeroth law: err on the side of awesome.**
- Magic shapes the world: economics, class, religion, warfare and ecology all respond to it.
  Ask what the society looks like because this power exists.
- Ecology drives culture. Strange weather or geography should change architecture, plants and
  animals, and daily habits.
- Use the iceberg. Show about a tenth of the lore. Hint at the rest through epigraphs, documents
  in the world and casual references.

## Structure: promises, progress, payoff

- **Promises.** The opening scene promises the book's tone, genre and type of conflict.
  Keep every promise, or deliberately subvert it with something better.
- **Progress.** Every chapter should visibly move a plot thread: a clue, a skill learned, a
  relationship changing, a plan advancing or failing. Readers forgive slow pacing when they can see progress.
- **Payoff.** Set up every climactic move in advance: the rule, the item, the skill, the
  flaw. A twist should feel surprising and inevitable at once. On a reread, the clues were in plain sight.
- **The avalanche.** In the last fifth of the book, all threads converge fast. Shorten chapters,
  switch POV more often, and pay off several setups one after another.
- **Multiple POVs.** Use two to four main POV characters on separate tracks that meet at
  the climax. Short interludes can show the wider world through minor characters.
- **Epigraphs.** Optional: a short in-world document at the head of each chapter that
  hints at the bigger mystery and sometimes contradicts what the characters believe.
- **Heist and plan scenes.** Lay out a plan, then let it go wrong in ways that test each
  character's skills.

## Character

- Every main character can be described with three sliders: **sympathy, proactivity and competence**. Start
  someone low on one slider and raise it over the book. Readers stay with a character who is
  rising. A likeable but passive character has to start acting. A brilliant jerk has to earn sympathy.
- Flaws are specific and structural: depression, arrogance, guilt, addiction,
  fear of leadership. The climax forces the character to face the flaw, not just to beat the villain.
- Growth is earned through repeated failure. The character breaks first, then makes a choice
  that shows the change. Magic or strength often scales with that inner growth.
- Villains have a coherent philosophy and believe they're right. Give them at least one point
  the reader agrees with.
- Use a found-family cast whose skills complement each other, and give each member a quirk and a dynamic with the others.

## Dialogue

- Banter reveals character and relationships. Each speaker should be recognizable without tags.
- Mostly "said". Use beats (small actions) instead of adverbs.
- Arguments in the dialogue are real ideological debates with good points on both sides,
  especially about duty, faith, honor or leadership.
- Characters explain magic or plans to each other naturally, for example teacher and student,
  rivals comparing notes, or a skeptic pushing back.

## Writing new material

1. Ask for or infer the premise, POV characters and magic or speculative element. If none is given,
   propose an original system first: power, cost, limits and effect on society, in a short table.
2. State the promise the opening will make.
3. Draft in window-pane prose, one POV per scene, with visible progress in every scene.
4. Before any climax, list the setups it pays off and confirm each one appears earlier.

## Rewriting existing text

1. Read the passage and name what it's doing: its POV, its goal, and the promise it makes.
2. Make these changes, in order:
   - Strip ornate or purple phrasing down to clear, specific language.
   - Tighten the POV, removing head-hopping and drifts into omniscient narration.
   - Make any magic or system concrete: what it does, what it costs, what it can't do.
   - Add one beat of progress if the scene has none.
   - Add character humor or banter where a scene is flat, but never in moments of grief.
   - Raise the proactivity slider: let the character choose and act instead of reacting.
3. Keep the author's plot, names, world and facts unless asked to change them.
   Change how it's told, not what happens.
4. Afterwards, list the main changes in 3-6 bullets (for example "cut 3 metaphors", "made the
   lightning cost explicit", "Ana now decides to open the gate").

## Final check

- [ ] Could a reader explain the magic's rules and costs from this text alone?
- [ ] Does every scene show progress?
- [ ] Is every climactic solution set up earlier?
- [ ] Is each POV voice distinct and limited to its own head?
- [ ] Is the prose clear rather than ornate?
- [ ] Is there no Sanderson-invented term, character or world?
